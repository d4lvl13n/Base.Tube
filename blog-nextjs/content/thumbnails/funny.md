dos:
  - Put one thing out of place in an otherwise normal scene.
  - Let the text comment on the image, not describe it.
  - Make the funny detail big enough to read at phone size.
  - Keep the punchline for the video; the thumbnail is the setup.
  - Leave the bottom-right corner clear for the video length.
donts:
  - Don't stack three jokes in one frame; the viewer gets none of them.
  - Don't explain the joke in the text.
  - Don't paste a famous meme photo or a real person's face; recreate the idea with your own image.
  - Don't make the scene dark or busy; comedy needs bright, clear light.

faq:
  - q: What makes a funny YouTube thumbnail work?
    a: >
      One thing out of place, shown clearly. The viewer's brain spots the
      mismatch (a raccoon at a desk, a suit in a bathtub) in a fraction of a
      second, and the click is them wanting to know how it happened. Add one
      face that reacts, or deliberately does not, and two or three words that
      comment on the scene.
  - q: How do I make a funny thumbnail without spoiling the joke?
    a: >
      Show the setup, not the ending. If the video is about painting a room in
      the wrong colour, show the man covered in paint, not the moment he
      explains it. A funny thumbnail should raise the question "how did that
      happen?". The video answers it. If the image already tells the whole
      story, there is no reason to watch.
  - q: What is a weird YouTube thumbnail, and should I use one?
    a: >
      A weird YouTube thumbnail uses a strange, uncomfortable or surreal image
      to stop the scroll. It can work for comedy and sketch channels, as long
      as the weirdness is one clear thing the eye can read at small size. A
      frame that is weird in five different ways looks like noise, and it
      should still match what happens in the video.
  - q: What is a "does he know" or "who is this" caption?
    a: >
      They are reaction captions borrowed from internet meme culture. A "does
      he know" thumbnail shows one person side-eyeing another who is clearly
      missing something. A "who is this" thumbnail shows someone squinting at
      an unexpected guest or object. They suit the side-eye layout above. Use
      them with your own images rather than the original meme photos.
  - q: Can I use meme images in my funny thumbnail?
    a: >
      Most meme images are photos or film frames that someone owns, and many
      show real people. Using them can lead to copyright claims or likeness
      issues. A safer route is to recreate the idea: your own face, your own
      props, or an original generated character. This page is not legal
      advice.

## A thumbnail is the setup, the video is the punchline

Comedy has a structure: a setup that creates a question, then a punchline that
answers it in a surprising way. A funny YouTube thumbnail only gets to do the
first half. It shows a situation that makes the viewer think "wait, why?",
and the video delivers the answer.

That is why the strongest comedy thumbnails often look calm. A man reading the
paper in a bathtub of spaghetti is funnier as a still image than the moment he
gets in, because the viewer fills in the story.

## One thing out of place

The easiest way to build a visual joke is a normal scene with one wrong
element:

- **Wrong place**: a canoe in a kitchen, a raccoon at a desk.
- **Wrong size**: a hamster as big as a bus, an elephant on a lap.
- **Wrong person for the job**: a grandmother on a skateboard, a dog flipping
  pancakes.
- **Wrong reaction**: total calm in total chaos.

Keep everything else ordinary. If the background is also strange, the one
wrong thing stops standing out. Bright, everyday places work best; our
[YouTube thumbnail backgrounds](/youtube-thumbnail-backgrounds) page has
simple options.

## Two kinds of faces

Funny thumbnails use faces in two opposite ways.

The **deadpan** face stays flat while the world around it falls apart. The
contrast is the joke, and it suits dry humour and sketches.

The **extreme** face is frozen mid-reaction: guilty, horrified, about to fall.
It suits fails, pranks and challenges. Pick one. A deadpan character next to
a screaming one in the same frame splits the attention.

The side-eye sits between the two: a small, suspicious glance that tells the
viewer something is off without saying what.

## Text that comments, not explains

The caption is the voice of the joke. Three styles work well:

- **Understatement**: "NORMAL DAY" over a scene that is anything but.
- **A question**: "WHO IS THIS?" next to a strange guest.
- **An excuse**: "IT WASN'T ME" from someone covered in cake.

What does not work is describing the picture. "MAN IN SPAGHETTI BATH" kills
the joke. Use heavy, rounded capitals with a thick outline; the
[YouTube thumbnail fonts](/youtube-thumbnail-fonts) guide lists free ones.

## Bright, flat, cheerful light

Lighting tells the viewer what kind of video this is before they read
anything. Dark shadows and moody colour grading say drama or horror. Comedy
reads best in even, bright light with saturated colours: yellow kitchens,
cyan skies, pink cushions. The one exception is the **Caught** layout, where
a harsh camera-flash look in a dark room is part of the joke, because it
looks like evidence.

## When to use each layout

- **Deadpan** for sketches and dry, absurd humour.
- **Caught** for fails, pranks and "I shouldn't have" stories.
- **Wrong scale** for edits and surreal sketches.
- **Side-eye** for reactions, roasts and "something is off" stories.
- **Animal in charge** for pet channels and animal comedy.

## Weird is fine, confusing is not

There is a difference between a weird thumbnail and an unreadable one. The
viewer should understand what is in the picture within a second, even if they
do not understand why. A joke that only works full-screen, or relies on a
small detail, disappears in a phone feed.

## Check it at phone size

Export at 1280 × 720 in 16:9 (the
[YouTube thumbnail size guide](/youtube-thumbnail-size) has the current
limits). Then look at it small, among other videos, in the
[YouTube thumbnail preview](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-preview). If the joke
still lands at that size, compare two versions in the
[thumbnail tester](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-tester). No tool can tell you in
advance how many people will click; the real answer comes from YouTube once
the video is live.
