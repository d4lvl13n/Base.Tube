## What an NBA thumbnail has to show

Basketball is one of the easiest sports to read in a single image. A ball, a
rim and a body in the air tell the whole story. The best NBA thumbnails use
that: they freeze the play at its highest point and add one or two words.
Everything else, from the crowd to the scoreboard, stays in the background.

The hard part is legal, not visual. Real players, team logos and real team
jerseys belong to other people. Every example on this page uses invented
athletes in invented colour pairs, such as coral and mint, lime and charcoal
or lavender and silver. Your thumbnail can look like the league without
copying any of it.

## Pick the peak frame

Watch the clip frame by frame and stop where the energy is highest: the
moment before the ball goes down through the rim, the ball halfway to the
basket at the buzzer, the defender already falling. A frame one second too
early or too late looks flat. This applies even if you make the image from
scratch: describe the peak, not the run-up.

## Five video types, five layouts

Basketball channels mostly publish five kinds of videos, and each needs a
different frame.

- **Highlight**: one athlete at the peak of the play, dark arena, bright
  lights.
- **Breakdown**: the court or a frozen play takes the frame, with yellow
  circles and white arrows, and a small presenter on one side.
- **Reaction**: a creator's face with one big emotion, the moment blurred
  behind.
- **Meme**: a funny moment exaggerated, flat bright colour, a red circle and
  a loud word.
- **Game build**: a video game style render with a rating card or attribute
  bars, for basketball video game channels (the 2K-style videos).

Keep one layout per series so returning viewers spot your uploads quickly.

## Breakdowns: draw on the play

Analysis videos sell an insight, so the thumbnail should show where to look.
Darken the frame and spotlight one player, draw the passing lane with a white
arrow, circle the mistake in yellow. Three marks at most. A court covered in
lines looks like homework; one circle looks like a secret.

## Memes: make your own face

Basketball meme culture is built on photos of real players crying, laughing
or staring. Those photos are not yours to use. The same formats work with
your own face, a friend, or a synthetic character: what makes them funny is
the exaggeration, not who is in them. Push it further than feels normal:
bigger eyes, more saturated colour, a thicker red circle.

## Text and colour

Use one to three words in capitals, in a tall condensed font with a thick
outline. "POSTER DUNK", "AT THE BUZZER" and "WHO WINS" all read at a glance.
The [YouTube thumbnail fonts guide](/youtube-thumbnail-fonts) lists free
fonts that hold up at small sizes.

For colour, let the ball do some of the work: its orange is already the
brightest thing on a court. Put it against a navy arena and use yellow only
for marks and text. Invent jersey colour pairs that do not match any real
team, and keep the jerseys plain, with no numbers or lettering.

## Backgrounds

A dark arena with flaring lights and a blurred crowd is the most reliable
background for highlights. Breakdowns work best on a clean top-down court.
Memes and reactions can use a flat bright colour. The
[YouTube thumbnail backgrounds guide](/youtube-thumbnail-backgrounds) shows
more options that keep the subject in front.

## Size and the final check

Export at 1280 × 720 pixels in a 16:9 frame; the
[YouTube thumbnail size guide](/youtube-thumbnail-size) has the current
limits. Keep the bottom-right corner clear for the video length. Then put
your thumbnail next to other videos in the
[YouTube thumbnail preview](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-preview) and compare
two versions with the [thumbnail tester](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-tester).
No tool can predict how many people will click before the video is live; the
real answer comes from YouTube once it is published.
