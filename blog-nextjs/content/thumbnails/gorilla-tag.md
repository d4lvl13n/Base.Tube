## What a Gorilla Tag thumbnail is up against

Gorilla Tag is a virtual reality game for Meta Quest, SteamVR and PlayStation
VR2. Players are legless gorillas, called monkes, who move by pushing off the
ground and trees with their arms. Its world is deliberately simple: low-poly
trees, flat colours, plain faces.

That simplicity is a challenge for thumbnails. A raw screenshot of the forest
looks almost the same as every other one in the search results. To stand out,
a Gorilla Tag thumbnail has to add what the game itself keeps minimal:
strong colour, a clear pose and a single story.

## Your monke's colour is your brand

Players choose the colour of their monke, and many creators keep the same one
in every video. Use that. A bright, single colour (lime, hot pink, ice blue,
yellow) is the easiest thing to spot in a small thumbnail, and returning
viewers learn to look for it. Keep the background in calmer greens and browns
so your monke is the brightest thing in the frame.

## The arms tell the story

Monkes have no legs, so the arms carry all the movement and most of the
emotion. Stretched forward means speed. Raised high means a win or a scare.
Hands on the head means a fail. A monke with its arms hanging down looks
static, even in an action video. Pick the pose before anything else.

## VR footage makes poor stills

Gameplay is recorded from the player's eyes, so most frames show two hands,
a blur of trees and the horizon tilting. That is great to watch and poor as a
still image. Plan the thumbnail separately: show your monke from the outside,
large, in the moment the video promises. Recreate the scene or generate it,
rather than hunting for one usable frame in shaky footage.

## Match the look to the video idea

Gorilla Tag videos fall into a few clear types, and each has its own look.
The five layouts on this page follow them. The examples are generated with
Base.Tube to show what a Gorilla Tag thumbnail can look like; they use the
game's look, its monkes, maps and cosmetics.

- **Horror maps and mods:** the Caves, the Basement or the Forest at night,
  one creature that does not belong and a monke in the light. Darkness
  stands out in a bright feed.
- **Meme moments:** one monke, one absurd object or situation, and a big
  reaction. A before and after split works well for glow-up videos.
  Cosmetics are bought with the game's Shiny Rocks currency, so a pile of
  them is an easy shorthand for a shop or spending video.
- **Crossovers:** Gorilla Tag mixed with another kind of game, such as a 2D
  platformer or a block world. Keep the monke recognisable so viewers know
  which game they are clicking on.
- **Real world:** the game meets real life, through mixed reality, a real
  gorilla or a creator reacting in a headset. If you show a face, show your
  own.
- **Hunts and hide and seek:** the chase itself or the hiding spot. In
  Infection mode, tagged players become lava monkes, so glowing orange
  already means "they're coming".

## Use the maps as ready-made palettes

Each map already has its own colours, and regular players recognise them
instantly. The Forest is green and brown, the Caves are dark grey and
shadowy, the Canyon is warm orange rock. Choose the map whose colours fit the
mood of the video, then push those colours a little further in the
thumbnail: deeper shadows for the Caves, hotter orange for the Canyon. That
keeps the scene familiar while making it stand out from plain screenshots.

## Text and backgrounds

Many viewers of this game are young and watch on phones or tablets. Keep the
text to three words in a heavy, rounded font with a thick outline; the
[YouTube thumbnail fonts guide](/youtube-thumbnail-fonts) lists options that
stay readable. For the scene behind your monke, see the
[YouTube thumbnail backgrounds guide](/youtube-thumbnail-backgrounds), and
remember the rule: simplify, then blur or darken.

## Size and checks

Upload at 1280 × 720 pixels (16:9). For file size and format limits, see the
[YouTube thumbnail size guide](/youtube-thumbnail-size). Keep the bottom-right
corner empty, where YouTube shows the video length. Then put your thumbnail
next to other videos in the
[YouTube thumbnail preview](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-preview) and compare
versions with the [thumbnail tester](/tools/youtube-thumbnail-tester). No tool
can tell you in advance how many people will click; that answer comes from
YouTube once the video is live.
